About Me

Alvi Ador. CSE student, security enthusiast, builder of things that have to actually work.

I'm a Computer Science & Engineering student with a specific obsession: software that holds up when someone actually tries to break it, not just when a demo goes well. This page is the full version: education, skills, what I've built, and what it actually took to build it.

Who I Am

Early-career, self-taught in most of the parts that matter, and allergic to claiming something works when I haven't checked.

I'm Alvi Ador, a Computer Science & Engineering student. My coursework covers the usual ground (data structures, software engineering, computer vision), but most of what I can actually do came from building things past the point where the assignment technically ended: finishing the hospital system's role-based access after the grade was already in, going back to add a CAPTCHA to a login form nobody asked for, rewriting an encryption flow three times until it was actually correct instead of just compiling.

I care about security specifically because it's the field where "looks like it works" and "actually works" are most dangerously far apart. A broken button is obvious. A broken access control check is invisible until someone finds it, and by then it's not your problem to fix quietly anymore. That gap is what pulled me from "full-stack student" toward "security-minded developer," and it's the lens I now run every project through.

I'm based in Bangladesh, early in my career, and genuinely still excited by the parts of this work that are supposed to get tedious: reading a spec instead of a tutorial, re-checking an edge case for the fifth time, asking "but what if someone sends something I didn't expect" one more time than feels necessary.

What I've Had to Push Through

The parts that didn't work on the first, second, or fifth try.

Cryptography that actually had to be correct. Reading about RSA-OAEP and AES-256-GCM is one thing. Implementing a flow where a browser wraps a one-time key with a public key, and getting the padding, the IV handling, and the key derivation all correct enough that it actually decrypts back to the original message took several wrong attempts that silently produced garbage instead of plaintext, and a lot of reading the actual Web Crypto API spec instead of the first Stack Overflow answer.

Learning a rendering pipeline with no safety net. Somapura Mahavihara was my first real computer graphics project, built directly on OpenGL instead of a game engine. Transformation matrices and camera math that look clean in a textbook are a lot less clean when your scene renders upside down, or a light source is technically correct and still makes everything look wrong. There's no abstraction layer to blame when that happens, just you and the math.

A penetration test with no hints. The security assessment gave me an IP address and nothing else. No known vulnerability to confirm, no walkthrough to follow. Just Nmap, a lot of reading about what a specific outdated FTP daemon actually was once I found it, and the slower, less glamorous work of confirming an exploit would actually work before trying it for real.

Training a model that had to generalize, not memorize. It's easy to get a CNN's training accuracy near 100%, which is usually a sign something's wrong. Getting training and validation curves to track each other instead of diverging meant actually understanding regularization and data splits, not just running more epochs and hoping.

Balancing coursework with projects nobody assigned. Most of what's above wasn't required for a grade. Building it anyway, on top of a full course load, meant a lot of late nights deciding a side project mattered more than finishing early, a trade I'd make again every time.

How I Work

Four habits that show up in everything I build.

Question First

Before writing a feature, I ask what happens when someone tries to misuse it. That question shapes the design, not a review that happens after.

Build It Real

If I claim something works, it has to actually run. I'd rather ship one real, working thing than describe ten hypothetical ones.

Read the Spec

Tutorials get you started. Getting cryptography, session handling, or a rendering pipeline actually correct means reading the real documentation, not just the first example that compiles.

Finish the Boring Part

Error handling, input validation, the fifth edge case nobody asks about: that's where most of my actual time goes, because that's where most real bugs live.
